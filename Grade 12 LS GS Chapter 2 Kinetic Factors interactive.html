2.1

Kinetic Factors: What They Are and How to Study Them

✔ Objectives of this chapter

  • Name the kinetic factors: surface area of a solid, temperature, catalyst, concentration.
  • Explain how each factor changes the rate, the half-life and the shape of the kinetic curves (reactant and product).
  • Distinguish homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis and describe the steps of heterogeneous catalysis.
  • Identify the factor that was changed from two kinetic curves.
  • Solve problems in the style of the official exams (CRDP).

◆ Kinetic factors

Kinetic factors are the factors that change the rate of a chemical reaction. The four factors of the course are:

  1. the surface area of a solid reactant (or of a solid catalyst),
  2. the temperature of the system,
  3. the catalyst,
  4. the concentration of a reactant.

2.1.1 Why do these factors change the rate? The collision idea

For two particles to react they must collide. The rate of a reaction depends on the number of effective collisions per second (collisions that lead to a reaction):

Figure 2.2 – More particles in the same volume, or faster particles, give more collisions per second.

★ The rate and the slope (reminder from Chapter 1)

A faster reaction gives steeper kinetic curves. The rate of disappearance of a reactant is the negative of the slope of the tangent or of the secant (−slope); the rate of formation of a product is the slope. When a factor increases the rate, the slopes are higher and the new curve is closer to the y-axis; the half-life t1/2 becomes shorter.

2.1.2 How to study one factor

★ The experiment must be repeated under the same conditions

To study one factor, the experiment is repeated changing only that factor. All the other conditions stay the same: the same volume, the same temperature, the same catalyst … so that the initial amounts and the maximum amounts of reactants and products are the same.

Exception: when the factor is the concentration (or the amount) of a reactant, the initial and the maximum amounts change (Section 2.5).

2.2

Factor 1: Surface Area of a Solid

★ Effect of the surface area

The rate increases when the surface area of a solid reactant increases (the solid is divided into smaller pieces). Examples: large pieces of charcoal burn slowly; small pieces burn faster; powdered charcoal burns at an explosive rate (the same is true for flour or coal dust).

Explanation: more particles of the solid are in contact with the other reactant, so there are more possible collisions per second and the rate becomes higher.

✔ Conclusion

A reaction goes faster with powdered reactants and slower with granulated reactants (or one big piece).

Experiment: the same mass (1 g) of zinc is added to the same volume of hydrochloric acid, 1 mol/L: Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl2 + H2, that is Zn + 2H+ → Zn2+ + H2. The bubbles of hydrogen are formed faster when the zinc is divided.

2.2.1 Kinetic curves: reactant and product, drawn separately

Figure 2.4 & Figure 2.5 – Left: mass of zinc (reactant). Right: volume of H2 (product) with initial rate tangents at t = 0 (powder: 37 mL/s, granules: 11 mL/s, strip: 4.4 mL/s).

★ What the graphs show

  • Reactant (mass of Zn): all the curves start at 1.00 g and end at 0; the curve of the powder falls faster, its |slope| is greater.
  • Product (volume of H2): all the curves end at the same maximum volume (367 mL); the curve of the powder rises faster.
  • The half-life t1/2 is shorter for the larger surface (the powder).

✎ Calculation

Maximum volume: n(Zn) = 1.00/65.4 = 0.0153 mol ⇒ n(H2)max = 0.0153 mol ⇒ V = 0.0153 × 24 = 0.367 L = 367 mL (molar volume 24 L/mol). The initial rate of formation of H2 for the powder (slope of the tangent) is about 37 mL/s, against 4.4 mL/s for the strip.

⚠ Safety

Fine powders (flour, coal, aluminum, sugar) mixed with air can burn explosively because their surface area is huge. Factories control dust for this reason.

2.3

Factor 2: Temperature

★ Effect of the temperature

  • When the temperature of the system increases, the rate of the reaction increases.
  • This is true for athermic, exothermic and endothermic reactions, reversible or irreversible.
  • Explanation: at a higher temperature the particles move faster: they collide more often and with more energy, so there are more effective collisions per second.
Interactive Half-Life & Rate Explorer • Figure 2.6

Reaction at Three Temperatures (T1 < T2 < T3)

Figure 2.6 – The same reaction at three temperatures (T1 < T2 < T3): reactant (left) and product (right) with their half-lives.

★ What the graphs show

  • At a higher temperature the reactant curve falls faster and the product curve rises faster: the slopes are greater and the curves are closer to the y-axis.
  • The half-life is shorter: here 23 min, 12 min, 6 min.
  • The initial and the maximum amounts are the same (the experiment is repeated with the same initial amounts, so [P]max = 0.40 mol/L for the three curves).

✎ Enrichment: a rough rule

For many reactions near room temperature, the rate roughly doubles (or more) for each rise of 10 °C. This is an approximate guide, not a law for every reaction.

2.3.1 Practical applications

Situation Explanation
Food is kept in a refrigerator or freezer low temperature slows down the reactions that spoil it
A pressure cooker cooks faster higher temperature speeds up the cooking reactions
In a titration, a sample taken from a reacting solution is cooled in an ice-water mixture the low temperature greatly slows (blocks) the reaction in the sample, so its composition stays the same at the chosen instant until the titration is finished
Hydrogen peroxide is kept in brown bottles, in a cool place to prevent the decomposition of H2O2, which is speeded up by light and heat

Level 3

The reaction S2O82− + 2I− → 2SO42− + I2 is followed by titration of the iodine formed. At each date, 10.0 mL of the mixture is poured into ice-cold water and titrated with a thiosulfate solution of concentration 0.010 mol/L: I2 + 2S2O32− → 2I− + S4O62−.

2.4

Factor 3: Catalysis

◆ The catalyst

A catalyst is a chemical or biochemical substance (an enzyme is a biological catalyst) that increases the rate of a reaction (it can make a very slow reaction fast enough to be observed) without being consumed: its chemical composition is conserved and it is recovered at the end of the reaction. It may undergo a physical change (for example it can become a fine powder). Only a small amount is needed, and it is written above the arrow of the equation, not among the reactants.

The change of the rate by a catalyst is called catalysis. Substances that increase the rate are positive catalysts (usually called simply catalysts); substances that decrease the rate are negative catalysts or inhibitors.

★ What a catalyst does and does not do

  • It increases the rate: the curves are steeper and the half-life is shorter (positive catalyst).
  • It does not change the initial amounts or the maximum (final) amounts of reactants and products: the plateau of the product is the same.
  • It is not consumed: it can be recovered and used again.
  • It is specific and selective: a catalyst speeds up a given reaction, and different catalysts can give different products from the same reactants.

2.4.3 Types of catalysis

Figure 2.11 – Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis.

◆ Homogeneous catalysis

The reactants and the catalyst are in one single phase (the same physical state). Examples:

  • Esterification (the reactants and the catalyst are all liquids): CH3COOH (l) + CH3OH (l) ⇌ CH3COOCH3 (l) + H2O (l) with H2SO4 (l) as the catalyst.
  • Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide in water with Fe3+ ions: 2H2O2 (aq) → 2H2O (l) + O2 (g), the catalyst Fe3+ (aq) is in the same aqueous phase.

◆ Heterogeneous catalysis

The reactants and the catalyst are in more than one phase (two or more). The catalyst is usually a solid and the reactants are gases or liquids. Examples:

  • Contact process: 2SO2 (g) + O2 (g) ⇌ 2SO3 (g) with solid V2O5 (s).
  • Hydrogenation of ethene: CH2=CH2 (g) + H2 (g) → CH3–CH3 (g) with solid nickel Ni (s).
  • Decomposition of hydrogen peroxide with solid MnO2: 2H2O2 (aq) → 2H2O (l) + O2 (g).
  • Car exhaust: 2CO (g) + 2NO (g) → 2CO2 (g) + N2 (g) on solid platinum and rhodium.

✔ Correction to remember

The reaction SO3 + H2O → H2SO4 does not need V2O5. The solid V2O5 is the catalyst of the oxidation of SO2 to SO3, 2SO2 + O2 → 2SO3. Also, ethene is CH2=CH2 (C2H4), written with a double bond.

2.4.4 Steps of heterogeneous catalysis

Interactive Catalytic Surface Animation • Figure 2.12

Hydrogenation of Ethene on Nickel Surface

Figure 2.12 – Hydrogenation of ethene on the surface of nickel: adsorption, reaction on the surface, desorption.

Step What happens
1. Adsorption the reacting particles stick to the active sites of the surface of the catalyst
2. Reaction the reaction takes place on the surface, between the adsorbed reactants (or an adsorbed reactant and a particle from the medium)
3. Desorption the products leave the solid surface; the sites are free again for new reactants

⚠ Adsorption in everyday life

A piece of activated charcoal placed in a refrigerator removes the unwanted odors: the molecules responsible for the smell are adsorbed on its huge surface. A solid catalyst is used finely divided for the same reason: a large surface has more active sites.

✎ Catalysis in industry and in life

  • Ammonia: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 with iron (Fe).
  • Margarine: hydrogenation of vegetable oils with nickel.
  • The body: enzymes such as catalase decompose H2O2 in the cells.
  • Inhibitors (negative catalysts) slow reactions: preservatives in food, stabilizers in H2O2 solutions.

2.5

Factor 4: Concentration of a Reactant

★ Effect of the concentration

  • When the concentration of a reactant increases, the rate increases (for gases, a higher pressure means a higher concentration). When the concentration decreases (for example by dilution), the rate decreases.
  • Explanation: more particles in the same volume give more collisions per second, so more reactions occur.
  • Contrary to the other factors, a change in the initial concentration changes the initial amount of the reactant, and therefore the maximum amount of the products.

Figure 2.13 – The reaction is carried out with two initial concentrations: reactant (left) and product (right). Dashed lines: tangents at t = 0.

★ What the graphs show

  • The reactant curve with the greater initial concentration starts higher and its tangent at t = 0 is steeper: the initial rate is greater.
  • The product curves have different plateaus ([P]max = 0.40 and 0.20 mol/L): the maximum amount is proportional to the initial amount of the limiting reactant.
  • The rate of formation of the product is also greater at the beginning for the higher concentration.

2.5.1 Why does the rate decrease during a reaction?

✔ Concentration and the variation of the rate with time

The rate is maximum at t = 0 and minimum at the end, where it becomes zero when the limiting reactant is totally consumed. The cause is the decrease of the concentrations of the reactants (fewer collisions). Graphically the slopes of the tangents of the kinetic curve start the greatest and then decrease with time (Chapter 1, Figure 1.11). When the slopes decrease, the rate decreases.
(It is not the increase of the concentration of the product by itself that makes the reaction slower.)

2.6.2 Which factor was changed? Reading two kinetic curves

Figure 2.15 – Two curves of a product: same plateau (left) or different plateaus (right).

★ Method

  1. Compare the plateaus (or the calculated maximum amounts).
  2. Same plateau: the initial amounts are the same, so the factor is the surface area, the temperature or a catalyst (the data of the problem tell which).
  3. Different plateaus: the initial amount of the limiting reactant is different: the factor is the concentration.
  4. The curve with the greater slopes (closer to the y-axis, shorter t1/2) corresponds to the greater rate.
  5. Count the factors: adding cold water changes two factors (concentration and temperature); immersing the beaker in a cold bath changes one (temperature).

2.6.6 Summary of the chapter

Topic Key points
Kinetic factors surface area of a solid, temperature, catalyst, concentration of a reactant
Effect on the curves a higher rate: steeper curves, closer to the y-axis, shorter t1/2
Rate and slope reactant: r = −slope; product: r = +slope (tangent or secant)
Surface area powder faster than granules or one piece; same maximum amount
Temperature higher temperature: higher rate for all reactions; cooling blocks a reaction before a titration; same maximum amount
Catalyst increases the rate, not consumed, selective, same maximum amount; inhibitor = negative catalyst
Catalysis types homogeneous: one phase; heterogeneous: solid catalyst; steps: adsorption, reaction, desorption
Concentration higher concentration: higher rate; different initial and maximum amounts
Rate with time maximum at t = 0, decreasing, zero at the end, because the concentrations of the reactants decrease

2.6.9 Exercise: Level 3 – Find the mistakes

Correct each statement.

  • (1) “The reaction SO3 + H2O → H2SO4 is catalyzed by V2O5.”
  • (2) “Ethene is CH2CH2 and its hydrogenation on Ni is a homogeneous catalysis.”
