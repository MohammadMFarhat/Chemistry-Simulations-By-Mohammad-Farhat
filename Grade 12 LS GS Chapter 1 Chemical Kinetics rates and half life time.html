1.1

What is the Rate of a Reaction?

1.1.1 The chemical system

A chemical reaction is a system in which reactants change into products: R → P. For the simplest reaction A → B, the amount of A decreases and the amount of B increases with time.

◆ Rate of a reaction

The rate of a reaction measures how fast the concentration (or the number of moles, or the mass, or the volume of a gas …) of a reactant or of a product change with time. It is always positive, like a speed. Its unit is mol·L−1·s−1 (or mol·L−1·min−1).

The rate can be defined for two kinds of species:

Species Name of the rate What happens to the concentration
a reactant rate of consumption (or disappearance) decreases
a product rate of formation (or appearance) increases
✎ Chemical kinetics

Chemical kinetics studies how fast reactions occur. In this chapter we follow reactions that are slow enough to be measured (seconds, minutes or hours), whether they are reversible or irreversible.

1.1.2 Tangent and secant to a curve

Figure 1.2 – A tangent touches the curve at one point; a secant cuts it at two points (M and N).
◆ Definitions
  • • The tangent to a curve at a point P is the straight line that touches the curve at P (it has the same direction as the curve at P). Its slope gives the rate at the instant of P.
  • • The secant MN is the straight line that cuts the curve at two points, M and N. Its slope gives the average rate between the two instants of M and N.
★ The rate and the slope

• Reactant: the curve is decreasing, so its slope is negative. The rate of disappearance is the opposite of the slope (the negative of the slope) of the tangent or of the secant, so that the rate is positive:

r(reactant) = −slope = −Δ[R]Δt

• Product: the curve is increasing, so its slope is positive. The rate of formation is equal to the slope:

r(product) = slope = Δ[P]Δt
⚠ Mistakes to avoid
  • • The rate is never negative. It is Δ[R] (not the rate) that is negative for a reactant.
  • • A kinetic curve is not a circle and has no radius: it is just a decreasing (reactant) or increasing (product) curve.
  • • A secant is a line that cuts the curve in two points, not “in two parts”.

1.1.3 Kinetic curves of a reaction

A kinetic curve is the graph of the concentration of a species as a function of time. Consider N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3 with the initial concentrations [H2]0 = 0.60 mol/L and [N2]0 = 0.20 mol/L (no ammonia at the start).

Figure 1.3 – Kinetic curves: the two reactants decrease, the product increases.
Inspect t = 15 min
✔ Reading the kinetic curves
  • • Every kinetic curve starts at t = 0, whatever the initial amount of the species (it can be 0 for a product that is not yet formed).
  • • The curves of the reactants are decreasing; the curve of a product is increasing.
  • • The reactant that is completely consumed first is the limiting reactant.

Reactant and product graphs, drawn separately, show the same information. The reactant tends to 0 at the end of the reaction; the product tends to its maximum value:

1.2

Stoichiometry of the Rates

★ Rates and the balanced equation

For the reaction a A + b B → c C + d D, the amounts that react or form are proportional to the stoichiometric coefficients. If the volume V is constant, the concentrations and therefore the rates follow the same proportions. The rate of the reaction is the same for all the species when each rate is divided by the coefficient of the species:

r(A)a = r(B)b = r(C)c = r(D)d

With the advancement x of the reaction, the rate of the reaction (at constant volume) is:

r = 1V × dxdt

For 3H2 + N2 → 2NH3 this gives:

r(H2)3 = r(N2)1 = r(NH3)2
Figure 1.5 – At the same instant (t = 10 min) the three tangents have different slopes: r(H2) : r(N2) : r(NH3) = 3 : 1 : 2.
✎ Check with the figure

0.0182/3 = 0.0061 = 0.0121/2 ✔. The species with the largest coefficient changes the fastest. (The signs are the signs of Δ[ ]: the rates themselves are positive.)

✔ Why the negative sign for a reactant?

Over the interval Δt, Δ[H2], Δ[N2] < 0 (they decrease) and Δ[NH3] > 0. To obtain positive rates we write r(R) = − Δ[R]/Δt for a reactant and r(P) = + Δ[P]/Δt for a product.

1.3

The Three Types of Rates

The rate of a species changes with time, so we must say at which time (or between which times) we measure it. There are three types of rates: initial, instantaneous and average.

Type Time Graphical tool Expression for a reactant
Initial rate at t = 0 tangent at t = 0 −(d[R]/dt)t=0
Instantaneous rate at one instant t tangent at t −(d[R]/dt)t
Average rate over an interval Δt = t2 − t1 secant MN −Δ[R]/Δt

1.3.1 a) The initial rate

The initial rate of formation of a product is the slope of the tangent drawn to the curve [P] = f(t) at t = 0. The initial rate of disappearance of a reactant is the opposite of the slope of the tangent to [R] = f(t) at t = 0.

★ Procedure (graph)
  1. Draw the tangent to the curve at t = 0.
  2. Choose two points A1(t1 ; y1) and A2(t2 ; y2) on the tangent (not on the curve).
  3. Calculate:
    r(P)t=0 = y2 − y1t2 − t1 r(R)t=0 = −y2 − y1t2 − t1
Figure 1.6 – Initial rates for N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3: formation of NH3 (left) and disappearance of H2 (right).

1.3.2 b) The instantaneous rate

The instantaneous rate at the instant t is the slope of the tangent to the curve at that instant (opposite of the slope for a reactant). It is the derivative of the concentration with respect to time:

r(P)t = d[P]dt r(R)t = −d[R]dt
★ Procedure (graph)
  1. At the given instant t, draw the tangent to the curve.
  2. Choose two points A1(t1 ; y1) and A2(t2 ; y2) on the tangent.
  3. Use the same formula as for the initial rate.
Figure 1.7 – Instantaneous rates at t = 20 min: formation of NH3 (left) and disappearance of H2 (right).

1.3.3 c) The average rate

The average rate over the interval Δt = t2 − t1 is the slope of the secant MN joining the points M(t1) and N(t2) of the curve (opposite of the slope for a reactant):

r(P)avg = [P]2 − [P]1t2 − t1 r(R)avg = −[R]2 − [R]1t2 − t1
★ Procedure (graph or table)
  1. Read on the graph (or in the table) the concentrations at t1 and t2: M(t1 ; [ ]1) and N(t2 ; [ ]2).
  2. Replace in the formula.
  3. Give the unit: concentration unit ÷ time unit.
Figure 1.8 – Average rates between t = 10 min and t = 30 min: formation of NH3 (left) and disappearance of H2 (right).
✎ Average and instantaneous rate

The average rate over a short interval is close to the instantaneous rate in the middle of the interval. Here the instantaneous rate of H2 at t = 20 min is 0.011 and its average rate between 10 and 30 min is 0.0115 (mol·L−1·min−1). If Δt → 0, the secant becomes the tangent.

✔ Check with the stoichiometry

From Figures 1.7 and 1.8: r(H2)/3 = 0.011/3 ≈ 0.0037 and r(NH3)/2 = 0.0074/2 = 0.0037 ✔ (instantaneous, t = 20 min). Average: 0.0115/3 ≈ 0.0038 and 0.0077/2 ≈ 0.0038 ✔.

1.4

Variation of the Rate with Time

★ The rate decreases with time

For a reaction that starts with reactants only, the rate is maximum at the beginning (t = 0) and minimum at the end, when the reactants are consumed (rate → 0). The slopes of the tangents, in absolute value, start the greatest and then decrease.

Figure 1.11 – Two tangents drawn at t1 = 5 min and t2 = 30 min: the slope is smaller at the later time, for the reactant (left) and for the product (right).
✔ Why does the rate decrease?

The rate depends on the concentrations of the reactants. As the reaction proceeds, the reactants are consumed, their concentrations decrease, so the rate becomes smaller. (The product curve is also less steep with time only because the reactants are being used up.)

✔ How to answer “describe the variation of the rate with time” in an exam
  1. Write the definition of the instantaneous rate and its expression, for example r(R)t = −(d[R]/dt)t = − slope of the tangent at t.
  2. Draw two tangents at two different instants t1 < t2.
  3. Compare the slopes (in absolute value): |slope at t1| > |slope at t2|.
  4. Conclude: the rate decreases with time, because the concentration of the reactant decreases.

✔ Quick Check – Test Yourself

# Question (try it first!) Answer

2 What is the rate at the end of a complete reaction? Zero (the curve is flat).
3 How do you show that the rate decreases? Draw two tangents at t1 < t2 and compare their slopes.
4 Why does the rate decrease? The concentrations of the reactants decrease.

1.6

Half-Life Time

◆ Half-life time (t½)

The half-life time of a reaction is the time needed to consume half of the initial amount of the limiting reactant; equivalently, the time needed for a product to reach half of its maximum value (the value reached when the limiting reactant is totally consumed).

[R]t½ = [R]02 [P]t½ = [P]max2
✎ If there is already some product at the beginning

If the product is present at the start, the half-life is reached at the half of the change: [P] at t1/2 = ([P]0 + [P]max)/2.

Figure 1.15 – Half-life from the curve of a reactant (left) and from the curve of a product (right).
★ Graphical method
  1. Reactant: calculate [R]0/2, mark it on the concentration axis, draw a horizontal line up to the curve, then a vertical line down to the time axis: that time is t1/2.
  2. Product: calculate [P]max/2 and do the same projections.
  3. The half-life has the unit of time.

1.6.1 Caution: the maximum amount of a product

To use a product curve, you must know its maximum value. Look at the end of the curve:

Figure 1.16 – Left: a plateau shows that the reaction has ended. Right: no plateau, so the maximum must be calculated.
★ Rule
  • • If the graph shows a plateau (or the table shows at least two equal values at different times), the reaction has ended: [P]max is the value of the plateau.
  • • If there is no plateau, the reaction is not finished. Calculate [P]max from the stoichiometry of the limiting reactant; never take half of the last value of the graph.

1.6.2 Example from the NO2 data

Figure 1.17 – t1/2 of the decomposition of NO2 found from the reactant and from both products.
✔ Solution

Reactant: [NO2]0 = 0.0100 ⇒ half = 0.0050 mol/L. The table gives 0.0055 (150 s) and 0.0048 (200 s), so t1/2 ≈ 185 s.

Product NO: NO2 is the only reactant, so if it were totally consumed [NO]max = 0.0100 mol/L (the curve has no plateau at 400 s). Half = 0.0050 mol/L ⇒ t ≈ 185 s (between 0.0045 at 150 s and 0.0052 at 200 s).

Product O2: [O2]max = 0.0100/2 = 0.0050 mol/L; half = 0.0025 mol/L ⇒ t ≈ 183 s, the same half-life.

A wrong method: half of the last value of [NO] (0.0069/2 = 0.00345) would give about 100 s. This is not the half-life.

1.6.3 When does the reaction end? The first half-life

Figure 1.18 – If a reaction ends after 200 min, its first half-life is much shorter than 100 min.
✔ Why?

The reaction is fastest at the beginning, when the concentrations of the reactants are greatest. So half of the reactant is consumed in a time shorter than half of the total duration. For a reaction that ends at 200 min, t1/2 < 100 min.

1.6.4 Factors that change the half-life

Figure 1.19 – At a higher temperature the reactant curve is steeper, the product curve rises faster and t1/2 is shorter.
★ Temperature and half-life

When the temperature is raised, the rate is higher, the reaction ends sooner and t1/2 is shorter; graphically the kinetic curve is steeper (closer to the vertical axis). A catalyst or a higher concentration also makes a reaction faster.
Everyday life: meat is kept in a freezer to slow down the reactions that spoil it; left outside in hot weather it spoils quickly.

✎ Half-life and concentration

The half-life is defined for given conditions (temperature, catalyst, initial amounts). For some reactions, such as the decomposition of H2O2 or of N2O5 studied here, successive half-lives are equal, but this is not a general law: do not state that the half-life never depends on the concentration.

1.7.7 Exercise: Level 3 – Find the mistakes

Level 3

A student writes:

“(1) The rate of disappearance of H2 in N2 + 3H2 → 2NH3 is Δ[H2]/Δt = −0.06, so the rate is negative.

(2) Then r(NH3) = r(H2) = 0.06.

✔ Solution
Solution hidden — click "Show Answer" to reveal

(1) The rate is positive: r(H2) = −Δ[H2]/Δt = +0.06; it is Δ[H2] that is negative.

(2) The rates are proportional to the coefficients: r(NH3) = (2/3) × 0.06 = 0.04.

(3) The instantaneous rate is given by the slope of the tangent (the secant gives the average rate).

(4) The half-life of a product is the time at which [P] = [P]max/2, where [P]max is the plateau value or the value calculated from the limiting reactant.
